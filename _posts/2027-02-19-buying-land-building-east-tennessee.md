Every month someone tells me they want to buy a few acres and build. It's a great plan — East Tennessee still has genuinely beautiful [land]({{ '/land-lots.html' | relative_url }}) within commuting distance — but the gap between a pretty parcel and a buildable one is where budgets go to die. Here's what I check before a client falls in love.

## Water in, water out

The first question on any unserved lot: where does sewage go? A failed perc test can make five acres nearly worthless for a home site, so we make the contract contingent on soil work. Then water in — utility tap, or a well — and what the power run from the road actually costs. On a long private drive, bringing utilities up can quietly rival the price of the land.

## The lot itself

A gorgeous sloped view lot can carry $60,000–$100,000 of site work the flat ugly one next door doesn't: cut-and-fill, retaining, a driveway that survives a Tennessee gully-washer, foundation engineering. As a licensed building contractor, I read grade, drainage, rock, and access the way most agents read a listing sheet — and I'll tell you what the dirt will cost before you offer, not after.

## Paper problems

Easements, shared-drive agreements, floodplain lines, county versus city restrictions, HOA build requirements on "estate lots" — every one of these is cheaper to discover during due diligence than at the building permit counter.

The right way to do this is backwards from the house: rough house plan, builder conversations, then the land that fits — a sequence I walk clients through in my [new construction]({{ '/new-construction.html' | relative_url }}) work.
