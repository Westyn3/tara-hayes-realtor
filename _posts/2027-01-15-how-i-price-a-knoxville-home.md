Every seller I meet has already seen a number — a Zestimate, a neighbor's sale, a cousin's opinion. Sometimes those numbers are close. But an algorithm has never walked your house, and in a market like Knoxville's, the difference between close and right is real money.

## Comps that actually compare

Pricing starts with closed sales, but the skill is in choosing them. A "comp" two streets over with a finished basement, a flat yard, and a new roof is not your comp if you have none of those. I pull the last six months of sales that genuinely match — square footage, condition, lot, school zone — and I adjust for the differences line by line, the way an appraiser will, because the appraiser is the second person your price has to convince.

## Condition, verified

This is where [my contractor's license]({{ '/blog/contractor-home-inspection-knoxville/' | relative_url }}) changes the math. I can tell you which $8,000 of work returns $20,000 at the closing table and which $20,000 returns nothing. Roof age, HVAC generation, water heater, panel — buyers' inspectors will price these against you, so we price them correctly first and take the surprise off the table.

## The market's mood this month

Knoxville's market isn't one market — [Farragut]({{ '/areas/farragut.html' | relative_url }}), [Powell]({{ '/areas/powell.html' | relative_url }}), and a Tellico lake house can be moving at three different speeds in the same week. Days-on-market and list-to-sale ratios in your zone, this season, set whether we price to spark competition or price to hold firm.

The goal isn't the highest list price — it's the highest closed price. Overpriced homes sit, then chase the market down and close under what honest pricing would have brought.
